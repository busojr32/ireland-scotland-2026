Your 16 handicap clears every ceiling here. Limits are 28–36 where they exist at all. Bring a printed GHIN record — several Irish clubs state "handicap required" and won't take your word for it.

You'll be renting clubs — a bag on the Ryanair Cork→Edinburgh leg isn't worth it. Reserve the set at the same moment as the tee time and state right or left hand; small clubs stock only two or three bags.

Always ask two questions when you bookClub competitions and society days can close a weekday at short notice, and early-to-mid September is when Irish clubs start hollow-coring greens — the single most likely thing to spoil a round, and never posted online. One call settles both, plus the club-hire price where it isn't published.

The rental car — read before booking

  • Credit cardMost US credit-card rental insurance specifically excludes the Republic of Ireland. It's the classic exclusion alongside Israel and Jamaica. Some cards (Chase Sapphire, certain World Elite Mastercards) do cover it — but you must bring a printed letter of coverage from your issuer, and Irish rental desks push back hard. Confirm in writing before you count on it.
  • CDWBasic CDW is built into every Irish rental and can't be declined. Excess is €1,500–3,000, with a €2,500–3,500 hold on your credit card. Super CDW is €8–15/day booked online vs €20–35 at the desk.
  • Never coveredTyres, glass, undercarriage — and driving on unpaved roads voids cover entirely. Your GPS will try to route you down gravel boreens in West Cork. Don't let it.
  • AutomaticScarce and pricey in Ireland. Book early. Worth every euro — you'll be shifting with your left hand otherwise.
  • RoadsRegional roads have stone walls and hedges right at the tarmac edge. Posted 80 km/h limits are aspirational. Budget 30–40% more time than Google Maps says. Jack Lynch Tunnel in Cork is free.
  • Alcohol50 mg/100 ml — stricter than most US states, and exceeding it voids all insurance.

Weather & packing

Mid-September is the sweet spot in both countries: shoulder season, crowds well down from August, ~13 hours of daylight. Dublin / Cork highs 16–18°C (61–65°F), lows 9–11°C. Edinburgh high 17°C, low 10°C, and windier. Roughly 17 rain days a month in all three — frequent light showers, not downpours.

  • A packable waterproof jacket with a hood. The single most important item.
  • Skip the umbrella. Atlantic and North Sea wind destroys them and locals don't bother.
  • Waterproof walking shoes with real grip — non-negotiable. You'll do 8–12 km a day, and Edinburgh's wet cobbles and Old Town steps are a genuine hazard in smooth soles.
  • Layers: t-shirt + fleece + shell. You'll use all three in one day.
  • One smart-casual outfit each for Glovers Alley / Bastion. No jacket required anywhere, but no athleisure.
  • Type G plug adapter — Ireland and the UK use the same UK-style 3-pin, so one type covers the whole trip. Not the European 2-pin.
  • Leap card for Dublin, or just tap contactless on DART/Luas/bus. Leap is not valid on Aircoach or Dublin Express.
  • Golf: bring your own shoes, glove and a printed GHIN record. Balls are cheaper bought at home.

Time zones & jet lag

Ireland and the UK are both +8 hours from San Diego and share the same clock, so there's no change between Cork and Edinburgh.

Flying east is the harder direction. On the plane, set your watch to Irish time at takeoff and sleep on their clock. On arrival day, daylight is the drug — do not lie down, even for twenty minutes. At 18:30 Dublin time your body thinks it's 09:30 AM, so you won't feel remotely sleepy; the risk is staying out too late, not nodding off. Aim to be in bed by 22:30 local.

🇮🇪 Corkfrom The Dean, Horgan's Quay

Order Beamish or Murphy's, not Guinness This matters in Cork in a way it doesn't in Dublin. Both are brewed in Cork — Murphy's on the Lady's Well site where James J. Murphy founded it in the 1850s, and both now under Heineken Ireland. Both brands are growing 28%.

Murphy's is the sweeter, softer, less-roasted of the three — chocolatey and easy. Beamish is drier and more roasted, closer to Guinness but fuller, and it's typically the cheapest pint in any Cork pub — about 50c under the rivals. Order Beamish and you'll drink well and cheaply.

Cork average pint: €6.13 — about 90c cheaper than Dublin. One amusing data point: the most expensive pint recorded in Cork (€8.50) is The Dean Bar, in your own hotel. Walk six minutes to the Shelbourne instead.

Three minutes away — and they share a corner

⭐ Café Royal Circle Bar3 min
19 West Register St · your local
The island-bar room with the Doulton tiled portrait panels of Victorian inventors — the finest pub interior in Edinburgh. Real ales, Scottish whiskies, and oysters at the bar; they still sell over a thousand Scottish rock oysters a week.

A piece of timing luck: 2026 is the Café Royal's bicentenary. Running all year — a '200 Years' seafood platter, a 200-year cider, a limited Stewart Brewing lager, and Georgian-to-Scottish-Heritage themed dinners. The separate Oyster Bar restaurant needs booking; the Circle Bar doesn't.
The Guildford Arms3 min
1 West Register St · next door
Spectacular high Victorian ceiling, ornate cornices, huge windows, and a serious real-ale bar with an upstairs gallery restaurant looking down over the room. Do these two back to back — they're on the same corner.

Folk and trad sessions

The Royal Oak12 min
1 Infirmary St · music every night
Nightly in the downstairs back bar from 21:00–21:30, and Sundays from about 17:00. The Sunday Wee Folk Club downstairs is mainly folk song and welcomes floor singers.
The room holds about thirty people — the most intimate folk room in the city. Arrive early or you're upstairs listening through the floor. Current start time and admission unverified — check their Facebook the week you travel.
Sandy Bell's18 min
25 Forrest Road · sessions since 1942
The folk pub of Edinburgh. Music every single night, plus most afternoons — roughly afternoons from 14:00 and evenings from 21:00. Free, no cover. The "Edinburgh Swing" fiddle style is native to this room.
The pub itself calls its schedule "a rough guide only" — turn up after 21:00 any night and there will be music, but don't plan around a specific act. One small room, no stage, standing once it fills; be there by 20:30 for a seat.
Captain's Bar15 min
4 South College St · gentle, unamplified
Traditional and contemporary song, fiddle-led, and unamplified — small enough that you're sitting a metre from the singer. Most nights from about 21:00 on a rotating schedule; the pub publishes no per-night listing, so check the noticeboard inside. Mon–Fri from 14:00, Sat from 13:00, both to 01:00.

Whisky and interiors

The Bow Bar15 min
80 West Bow · noon–midnight daily
400+ single malts, three regular and five changing cask beers mostly from independent Scottish breweries, and seven traditional Scottish air-pressure founts — more than any other pub anywhere. Grade C listed; one room, brewery mirrors, enamel signs.
Correction to what I told you earlier: this is not a music venue. CAMRA describes it as "quiet" and any session is a rare, informal weekend-afternoon thing. Go for the whisky.
Gourmet pies 12:00–17:00 only, then almost no food. Step at the entrance, no accessible toilet, and no table service.
Bennets Bar25 min
8 Leven St, Tollcross · 1891, Grade B listed
The one to make the walk for. 120 single malts, and it has the last surviving original gantry with four spirit casks in Edinburgh — four restored Aitken founts, hand-painted allegorical figures in tiled-and-mirrored arcading, two working brass water taps, and a marble spittoon trough at the bar.
Also a rare intact "jug bar": a snug with its own street entrance and serving hatch, from the days when women bought carry-out beer without entering the pub. CAMRA rates it the city's second-finest interior after the Café Royal. Next to the King's Theatre.
The Oxford Bar18 min
8 Young St · no music, no food
Ian Rankin's own pub and John Rebus's fictional one — Rankin still drinks here. It is deliberately, almost aggressively plain: two small bare rooms, pies at most, and locals who won't be impressed that you've read the books. That's the point. The front room fits maybe a dozen standing.

Worth the journey

The Sheep Heid Inn40–45 min
43–45 The Causeway, Duddingston
Claims 1360 and the title of Scotland's oldest surviving licensed premises; Mary, Queen of Scots and Queen Victoria are both associated with it. The 19th-century two-lane skittle alley is still playable — and you must book it.
The walk is the reason to go: route it through Holyrood Park under Arthur's Seat and around Duddingston Loch rather than along roads. Book a table — it's a destination Sunday-lunch pub and turns walk-ins away.
The King's Wark15 min by bus
36 The Shore, Leith · this one's for the food
15th-century origins, bare stone and beams on the Leith waterfront, and one of the better gastropub kitchens in the city — strong on seafood, with a well-known weekend brunch. Five minutes from Britannia, which is why it's your Saturday dinner. Book for dinner and for weekend brunch.

  • Fri Sept 18

    Cork    Cork Folk Festival — 40+ venues and a printed pub trail map so you can walk between sessions in one evening, plus a free opening event on Grand Parade. Dates not reliably verified — the official site is years stale. Historically the last weekend of September into the first of October, so it may just catch the end of your trip. Worth re-checking.
